\begin{abstract}
\normalsize
\noindent
Quantile forecasts for nonlinear time series require flexible models of temporal
dependence. We develop quantile deep echo state networks (Q--DESN), which
combine nonlinear temporal features with Bayesian quantile regression.
Conditional on a specified randomly generated network, the quantile location
is linear in regression coefficients under ridge or regularized-horseshoe
priors. Asymmetric Laplace and extended asymmetric Laplace likelihoods support
single-level regressions and a multiquantile deep echo state network
(MQ--DESN) that shares information by shrinking adjacent slope differences.
Posterior computation
uses Markov chain Monte Carlo and model-specific variational approximations.
Two simulation studies examine quantile-path recovery, forecast loss, and
quantile ordering. Q--DESN attains competitive forecast loss relative to
structural dynamic quantile linear models; multiquantile shrinkage reduces
crossings without uniform score gains. A retrospective streamflow application
combines gauge records with Global Flood Awareness System ensembles to study
quantile-specific forecast correction. An electricity-price application compares
region-specific regressions with matched forecasts from the pretrained neural
model PriceFM.
\textit{[PriceFM findings to be updated.]}
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textit{Key words:} Asymmetric Laplace likelihood; Bayesian shrinkage;
probabilistic forecasting; reservoir computing; variational inference.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Conditional quantiles describe tail risk and provide forecasts suited to
asymmetric decision losses and prediction intervals
\citep{KoenkerBassett1978,Koenker2005,Gneiting2011}. For a time series,
these forecasts depend on the observed response history and available
covariates, and can change with both the forecast origin and the lead time.
Let \(Y_{t+h}\) be the response \(h\) steps after time \(t\), and let
\(\mathcal F_t\) denote the information available then. Its level-\(p\)
conditional quantile is
\[
\text{Forecast quantile:}\quad
Q_{t,h}(p)=\inf\{u:\Pr(Y_{t+h}\le u\mid\mathcal F_t)\ge p\},
\qquad 0<p<1.
\]
To represent nonlinear dependence in these quantiles, we introduce the
quantile deep echo state network (Q--DESN): Bayesian quantile regression on
temporal predictors generated by a specified deep echo state network (DESN)
\citep{GallicchioMicheliPedrelli2018DeepESNDesign}. Its internal weights are
randomly drawn and held fixed before regression estimation. Conditional on
the resulting predictors, the quantile location is linear in the coefficients
of the final regression layer, called the readout. Nonlinear temporal
dependence is therefore represented through the predictors while inference
retains a regularized linear-regression structure.

Echo state networks (ESNs) generate such predictors by recursively transforming
inputs and preceding states under fixed internal weights
\citep{Jaeger2001EchoState,LukoseviciusJaeger2009ReservoirComputing}.
The collection of recurrent states is called a reservoir. Stacking reservoirs,
so that one layer supplies inputs to the next, gives a DESN
\citep{GallicchioMicheliPedrelli2018DeepESNDesign}. Bayesian regression on
reservoir features has precedent in the hierarchical Bayesian DESN of
\citet{McDermottWikle2018DeepESNUQ}, developed for spatiotemporal forecasting.

Quantile reservoir methods also have precedent.
\citet{LvZhaoLiuWang2016QRESNE} estimate ESN output weights by quantile
regression and combine quantile fits in a bootstrap ensemble for confidence
and prediction intervals. \citet{BonasWikleCastruccio2024CalibratedDeepESN}
first fit DESN point forecasts by ridge regression, then jointly estimate
lead-dependent residual quantiles with penalized quantile sheets and
calibrate the resulting intervals. These approaches establish quantile
reservoir forecasting and simultaneous residual-quantile estimation as
precedents; our contribution concerns the response-quantile regression,
its Bayesian regularization, and its posterior computation.

Dynamic quantile linear models (DQLMs) and extended dynamic quantile linear
models (exDQLMs) represent temporal dependence through evolving linear states
\citep{GoncalvesMigonBastos2020DQLM,barata2021_flex_quantile}.
\citet{DeLeonPradoSanso2026HydrologicProducts} combine hydrologic products
through evolving linear quantile states and source-specific discrepancies,
fitting levels separately before predictive synthesis. Q--DESN instead
represents temporal dependence through recurrent nonlinear predictors rather
than evolving linear coefficients.

We model response quantiles directly using asymmetric Laplace (AL) or
extended asymmetric Laplace (exAL) likelihoods, with ridge or
regularized-horseshoe (RHS) priors on the readout slopes. The multiquantile
deep echo state network (MQ--DESN) extends this regression by shrinking
adjacent quantile-specific slope differences, adapting the
Quantile-Varying Parameter construction of \citet{KohnsSzendrei2025QVP}.
This shares information across levels while allowing their effects to differ.
Interval forecasts use pairs of estimated response quantiles without an
additional residual-calibration regression or bootstrap ensemble. We derive
augmented Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) updates and model-specific
variational Bayes (VB) approximations for these regressions; the AL case has
standard full conditional distributions, including for MQ--DESN.

Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:forecast} introduce the model, posterior
computation, and forecast evaluation. The first simulation in
Section~\ref{sec:simulation} asks whether Q--DESN can reproduce forecast
behavior under the linear structural dynamics used by DQLMs and exDQLMs.
The second compares independent and multiquantile regressions on a common
seven-level grid. The applications in Sections~\ref{sec:data} and
\ref{sec:pricefm-application} consider streamflow ensemble correction and
electricity-price forecasting. Derivations and additional evidence are in
the supplement.

\section{Fixed-Feature Bayesian Quantile Models}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Single-level quantile regression}

Let \(Y_t\), \(t=1,\ldots,T\), be the response, with observed value
\(y_t\), and let
\(\vect x_t=(1,\tilde{\vect x}_t^\top)^\top\in\R^{r+1}\) be a specified
vector containing an intercept and \(r\) temporal predictors. Write
\(\vect\beta=(\beta_0,\vect\beta_{\mathrm{slope}}^\top)^\top\in\R^{r+1}\).
At probability level \(p_0\in(0,1)\), the linear quantile regression is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Quantile location:}\quad &\mu_t=\vect x_t^\top\vect\beta,\\
\text{Likelihood:}\quad
&Y_t\mid\vect x_t,\vect\beta,\sigma,\gamma
  \sim\exAL_{p_0}(\mu_t,\sigma,\gamma),\qquad t=1,\ldots,T.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:qdesn-marginal-main}
\end{equation}
Here \(\exAL_{p_0}\) is the quantile-fixed generalized asymmetric Laplace
family of \citet{yan2025new}. Its location \(\mu_t\) is the conditional
\(p_0\)-quantile, \(\sigma>0\) controls scale, and the shape parameter
\(\gamma\in(L,U)\) allows skewness and tail behavior to vary while preserving
that quantile. The endpoints satisfy \(L<0<U\) and depend on \(p_0\);
the supplement gives their definition and the density. Setting \(\gamma=0\)
recovers AL quantile regression
\citep{yu2001bayesian,KozumiKobayashi2011Gibbs}. We assign
\(\sigma\sim\IG(a_\sigma,b_\sigma)\), with
\(a_\sigma,b_\sigma>0\), independently of a proper shape-prior density
\(\pi_\gamma\) supported on \((L,U)\). AL fixes shape at zero and omits
its prior. Numerical prior settings are specified with each analysis.
Section~\ref{subsec:desn-features} constructs
\(\vect x_t\) from the response
history and covariates. Conditional on that design, the location in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qdesn-marginal-main} is linear in \(\vect\beta\).

\subsection{Deep echo state network features}
\label{subsec:desn-features}

Let \(\vect z_t\in\R^q\) contain specified predictive covariates and let
\(\vect u_t=(y_{t-1},\ldots,y_{t-m},\vect z_t^\top)^\top\in\R^{m+q}\)
combine them with \(m\) response lags. In a network with \(D\)
layers, the reservoir state
\(\vect h_{t,d}\in\R^{n_d}\) is a recurrent nonlinear transformation of
these inputs. For a square matrix \(\mat W\), write \(\rho(\mat W)\)
for its largest absolute eigenvalue. A sparse drawn matrix \(\mat W_d\)
with nonzero radius is scaled as
\(\bar{\mat W}_d=\rho_d\mat W_d/\rho(\mat W_d)\), where \(\rho_d\)
is the selected radius;
\(\mat W_d^{\mathrm{in}}\) is a fixed input matrix, and \(\alpha_d\) is the
leak rate. Between layers, the dimension-reduction matrix \(\mat Q_{d-1}\) gives
\(\tilde{\vect h}_{t,d-1}=\mat Q_{d-1}\vect h_{t,d-1}\).
The nonlinear activation function \(f\) acts componentwise, and \(k\) is a chosen feature
transformation.
With a fixed first-layer input bias \(\vect b_1^{\mathrm{in}}\), the
recursion and general feature vector are
\begin{equation}
\begin{alignedat}{2}
\text{First layer:}\quad &\vect h_{t,1}
&&=(1-\alpha_1)\vect h_{t-1,1}\\
&&&\quad+\alpha_1 f(\bar{\mat W}_1\vect h_{t-1,1}
+\mat W_1^{\mathrm{in}}\vect u_t+\vect b_1^{\mathrm{in}}),\\
\text{Higher layers:}\quad &\vect h_{t,d}
&&=(1-\alpha_d)\vect h_{t-1,d}\\
&&&\quad+\alpha_d f(\bar{\mat W}_d\vect h_{t-1,d}
+\mat W_d^{\mathrm{in}}\tilde{\vect h}_{t,d-1}),\quad d=2,\ldots,D,\\
\text{Reduced states:}\quad &\tilde{\vect h}_{t,d}
&&=\mat Q_d\vect h_{t,d},\quad d=1,\ldots,D-1,\\
\text{Features:}\quad &\tilde{\vect x}_t
&&=\big[\vect h_{t,D}^\top,k(\tilde{\vect h}_{t,1})^\top,\ldots,
k(\tilde{\vect h}_{t,D-1})^\top,\vect u_t^\top\big]^\top.
\end{alignedat}
\label{eq:desn-hierarchy}
\end{equation}
The fixed matrix \(\mat Q_d\) has dimension \(\tilde n_d\times n_d\).
Here \(\bar{\mat W}_d\) is \(n_d\times n_d\),
\(\mat W_1^{\mathrm{in}}\) is \(n_1\times(m+q)\), and
\(\mat W_d^{\mathrm{in}}\) is \(n_d\times\tilde n_{d-1}\) for \(d>1\),
and \(\vect b_1^{\mathrm{in}}\in\R^{n_1}\).
A small leak rate retains more of the previous state. If \(k\) preserves
dimension, the displayed feature vector
has dimension \(r=n_D+\sum_{d<D}\tilde n_d+m+q\); a dimension-expanding
transformation changes this count. Appending the intercept gives \(\vect x_t\).
Reservoir and input weights are drawn before estimating the regression
parameters and then held fixed. Candidate specifications can differ in these
draws and the other settings, as described in Section~\ref{sec:selection}.
A washout excludes initial states to reduce dependence on their chosen
starting values.

Equation~\eqref{eq:desn-hierarchy} permits direct inputs as well as reservoir
features. The multiquantile simulation and streamflow application use an
intercept and reservoir features only; their selected layer transformations
and the single-level designs are specified in the supplement. Conditional on
the observed fitting inputs, \(\mat X=(\vect x_1,\ldots,\vect x_T)^\top\)
is fixed. Future predictor vectors depend on the information and recursive
forecast construction in Section~\ref{sec:forecast}.

\subsection{Coefficient regularization}
\label{subsec:coefficient-regularization}

We regularize the slopes separately from the intercept, assigning
\(\beta_0\sim\Normal(b_0,V_0)\), with \(V_0>0\). Prior calibration is
fixed before the corresponding model comparison; it is distinct from
numerical starting values. For the \(r\) slopes, ridge regularization uses
\(\vect\beta_{\mathrm{slope}}\sim
\Normal(\zeros{r},\kappa_\beta^2\Id{r})\), with common prior standard
deviation \(\kappa_\beta\). The adaptive alternative uses the
Nishimura--Suchard product form of the regularized horseshoe
\citep{CarvalhoPolsonScott2010HS,PiironenVehtari2017RHS,
MakalicSchmidt2016SimpleSampler,NishimuraSuchard2023SSS}. Let
\(\Theta_\beta=(\vect\lambda^2,\vect\nu,\tau^2,\xi,\zeta^2)\)
collect its scale and auxiliary variables. The product prior is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Product prior:}\quad
&\pi_{\mathrm{NS}}(\vect\beta,\Theta_\beta)
 \propto\Normal(\beta_0\mid b_0,V_0)\,p(\Theta_\beta)\\
&\qquad\times\prod_{j=1}^r
 \Normal(\beta_j\mid0,\tau^2\lambda_j^2)
 \Normal(0\mid\beta_j,\zeta^2),\\
\text{Local scales:}\quad
&\lambda_j^2\mid\nu_j\sim\IG(1/2,1/\nu_j),\qquad
 \nu_j\sim\IG(1/2,1),\\
\text{Global scale:}\quad
&\tau^2\mid\xi\sim\IG(1/2,1/\xi),\qquad
 \xi\sim\IG(1/2,1/\tau_0^2).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rhs-product-main}
\end{equation}
Here \(p(\Theta_\beta)\) is the product of the displayed auxiliary
densities and the slab factor. The squared slab scale \(\zeta^2\) is
either fixed or assigned \(\IG(a_\zeta,b_\zeta)\); the studies specify
which. The inverse-gamma convention is
\(p(u\mid a,b)\propto u^{-a-1}\exp(-b/u)\).
Each Gaussian factor retains its scale-dependent normalizer. Conditional
on the scales, the \(j\)th slope therefore has variance
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Slope prior:}\quad
&\beta_j\mid\tau,\lambda_j,\zeta\sim\Normal(0,V_j),\\
\text{Effective variance:}\quad
&V_j=\left(\zeta^{-2}+\tau^{-2}\lambda_j^{-2}\right)^{-1},
\qquad j=1,\ldots,r.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rhs-var}
\end{equation}
The slab bounds the conditional coefficient variance, whereas local scales
allow slopes to escape global shrinkage. The Gaussian product also contributes
a scale-dependent factor, so replacing it by the normalized conditional
\(\Normal(0,V_j)\) with independent half-Cauchy scales would define a
different joint prior. The supplement gives this factor and the full
conditional calculations. A proper coefficient prior does not require
sample-orthogonal intercept and slope columns; any centering of the final
features must nevertheless be accompanied by the corresponding intercept
and prior transformation.

The fixed hyperprior scale \(\tau_0\) controls prior shrinkage through the
random global scale \(\tau\). For standardized predictors, a Gaussian
reference is \(\tau_{0,\mathrm{ref}}=
\{m_0/(r-m_0)\}\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}/\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{fit}}}\), where
\(m_0\in(0,r)\) is a prior reference number of slopes expected to escape
strong shrinkage, \(N_{\mathrm{fit}}\) is the fitting-sample size, and
\(\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is a Gaussian residual standard deviation
\citep{PiironenVehtari2017RHS}. This calibrates a prior scale, not a
starting value. Its derivation and likelihood-specific alternatives are in
the supplement.

\subsection{Multiquantile regression (MQ--DESN)}
\label{subsec:joint-qdesn}

Independent fits at \(K\) ordered levels \(0<p_1<\cdots<p_K<1\) assign a
separate quantile regression to each level. Their estimated curves can cross:
at some predictors, a lower-level estimate exceeds a higher-level estimate.
MQ--DESN shares information across levels to encourage coherent ordering,
using level-specific intercepts \(\beta_{0k}\) and slopes
\(\vect\beta_k\in\R^r\) on the same non-intercept feature vector
\(\tilde{\vect x}_t\) defined above:
\begin{equation}
\text{Quantile location:}\quad
\mu_{k,t}=\beta_{0k}+\tilde{\vect x}_t^\top\vect\beta_k,
\qquad k=1,\ldots,K.
\label{eq:joint-qdesn-readout}
\end{equation}
Let \(\vect y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)^\top\) be the observed response vector,
\(\theta\) collect the regression, likelihood, and prior-scale parameters,
and \(\pi(\theta)\) denote their prior. Write \(f_{p_k}\) for the exAL
density at level \(p_k\), with scale \(\sigma_k\) and shape \(\gamma_k\).
AL fixes every \(\gamma_k=0\) and omits the shape priors. The composite
update is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Composite likelihood:}\quad
&L_c(\theta;\vect y,\mat X)
 =\prod_{t=1}^T\prod_{k=1}^K
 f_{p_k}(y_t\mid\mu_{k,t},\sigma_k,\gamma_k),\\
\text{Composite posterior:}\quad
&\pi_c(\theta\mid\vect y,\mat X)
 \propto\exp\!\left\{-\ell_c(\theta;\vect y,\mat X)\right\}\pi(\theta).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:joint-composite-posterior-main}
\end{equation}
Here \(\ell_c(\theta;\vect y,\mat X)=-\log L_c(\theta;\vect y,\mat X)\)
is the negative composite log-likelihood. The posterior is defined when its
normalizing constant is finite.
Each factor is a normalized density, but their product reuses the same
response across levels and is not a normalized scalar-response sampling
density. The prior couples levels by regularizing the first slope vector
and adjacent differences,
\[
\text{Anchor and differences:}\quad
\vect\Delta_1=\vect\beta_1,\qquad
\vect\Delta_k=\vect\beta_k-\vect\beta_{k-1},\quad k=2,\ldots,K.
\]
Each block uses the product RHS prior in
Section~\ref{subsec:coefficient-regularization}, with its own global and
local scales. The slab policy and prior reference scales are fixed for the
comparison. Intercepts have separate Gaussian priors, restricted to
\(\beta_{01}\le\cdots\le\beta_{0K}\) when ordered intercepts are specified.
This is the intercept treatment used in the multiquantile simulation;
its VB approximation optimizes them subject to that ordering, whereas MCMC
samples their truncated Gaussian conditionals.
When \(K=1\), the hierarchy
recovers the single-level Q--DESN slope prior under RHS regularization.

The shrinkage acts on how predictor effects change across levels. The adjacent
location gap is
\[
\text{Adjacent location gap:}\quad
\mu_{k,t}-\mu_{k-1,t}
=(\beta_{0k}-\beta_{0,k-1})
+\tilde{\vect x}_t^\top(\vect\beta_k-\vect\beta_{k-1})
\]
so small adjacent slope differences reduce the predictor-dependent variation
around the intercept gap. With ordered intercepts this favors nearly parallel,
ordered curves while allowing quantile-specific effects. It does not constrain
the displayed gap to be nonnegative. Noncrossing constraints and monotone
quantile-process models impose ordering more directly
\citep{WuLiu2009StepwiseNCQR,BondellReichWang2010NoncrossingQR,
YangTokdar2017JointQuantilePlanes}.

When an ordered estimated grid is required, common post-estimation approaches
are rearrangement by sorting and isotonic projection
\citep{BarlowBrunk1972IsotonicDual,chernozhukov2010,
DeLeonPradoSanso2026HydrologicProducts}. For the raw forecast vector
\(\hat{\vect q}=(\hat q_1,\ldots,\hat q_K)^\top\), the simulation reporting
rule uses the equal-weight least-squares projection
\begin{equation}
\text{Ordered grid:}\quad
\vect q^*=\argmin_{z_1\le\cdots\le z_K}
\sum_{k=1}^K(\hat q_k-z_k)^2.
\label{eq:isotonic-projection-main}
\end{equation}
Projection changes estimated values and generally differs from sorting.
Its equal adjustment weights are distinct from the forecast-scoring weights.
Raw crossings are evaluated before this transformation. The supplement gives
the complete prior, its precision matrix, and additional ordering diagnostics.

\section{Posterior Computation}
\label{sec:inference}

Conditional on the design, the posterior combines the likelihood in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qdesn-marginal-main} with the coefficient, scale, and shape
priors. For MQ--DESN, the composite likelihood and adjacent-difference prior
each enter once, giving Eq.~\eqref{eq:joint-composite-posterior-main}.
For computation, exAL admits the equivalent
augmentation
\begin{equation}
\begin{alignedat}{2}
\text{Latent variables:}\quad
&v_t\mid\sigma&&\sim\Exp(\mathrm{rate}=1/\sigma),
\qquad s_t\sim\TN(0,1),\\
\text{Gaussian conditional:}\quad
&Y_t\mid\cdot&&\sim\Normal\!\left(
\vect x_t^\top\vect\beta+D_{p_0}(\gamma)\sigma s_t
+A_{p_0}(\gamma)v_t,
B_{p_0}(\gamma)\sigma v_t\right).
\end{alignedat}
\label{eq:qdesn-aug}
\end{equation}
The supplement's ``Distributional Conventions'' section defines the
probability-specific functions \(A_{p_0}\), \(B_{p_0}\), and \(D_{p_0}\);
\(D_{p_0}\) is the latent-shift coefficient. Here \(\TN(0,1)\) denotes a
standard Normal distribution truncated to \((0,\infty)\), and the second
Gaussian argument is a variance.
Integrating out \((s_t,v_t)\) recovers the marginal exAL model. For AL,
shape is fixed at zero and the latent shift term is absent.

Conditional on latent variables and prior scales, coefficient blocks have
Gaussian full conditionals. Ordered intercepts have Gaussian conditionals
truncated between neighboring intercepts. The product RHS prior gives
inverse-gamma updates for its squared scales and auxiliaries, with global
shape \((r+1)/2\) for \(r\) penalized slopes. These updates apply separately
to the first slope block and adjacent differences in MQ--DESN.
For AL, the \(v_t\) full conditional is generalized inverse Gaussian (GIG)
and the scale full conditional is
\(\IG(a_\sigma+3T/2,b_\sigma^*)\), where the supplement gives the residual
sum in \(b_\sigma^*\). The \(3T/2\) term is specific to conditioning on
the displayed \(v_t=\sigma u_t\) augmentation. Thus standard full
conditional laws are available for AL even in the multiquantile regression;
the implemented MQ sampler uses log-scale slice transitions for its GIG block.

For exAL, \(v_t\) remains GIG and \(s_t\) has a positive truncated-Normal
conditional. Scale conditional on shape is also GIG, but shape requires a
numerical update. The scale-collapsed transition used in the MQ simulation
first integrates positive scale out of the joint scale--shape kernel,
then updates shape under that marginal kernel, and finally draws scale
conditional on the new shape and the other blocks. This removes scale from
the shape transition without discarding its uncertainty. It is distinct from
a variational quadrature calculation.

Posterior simulation becomes costly when high-dimensional regressions must
be fitted repeatedly. VB instead chooses a tractable distribution by iterative
updates of its factors. Here Gaussian coefficient factors and inverse-gamma
shrinkage factors retain their analytic updates. For AL the remaining factors
are conjugate; for exAL the scale--shape factor requires an approximation.
The generic Laplace--Delta calculation of \citet{wang2013nonconjugatevb}
uses a Gaussian approximation on transformed coordinates. The MQ simulation
instead retains a structured factor
\(q(\gamma_k)q(\sigma_k\mid\gamma_k)\): scale is integrated analytically,
and one-dimensional quadrature over shape gives its normalizer and mixed
moments. These are different approximations, not interchangeable names.

The MQ Gaussian slope factor retains covariance across quantile levels,
while its ordered intercepts are constrained point estimates. Its reported
variational uncertainty therefore excludes intercept uncertainty. Numerical
stopping is assessed from the stated coordinate monitor and parameter changes;

\section{Forecasting, Scoring, and Model Selection}
\label{sec:forecast}

\subsection{Multi-step quantile forecasts}

A forecast origin \(o\) is the last time included in its observed history
and may follow the fitting endpoint \(T\). Horizon \(h\) refers to
\(Y_{o+h}\). The simulation evaluations retain the fitted parameter
distribution while incorporating newly observed responses into the states
at subsequent origins; they do not refit the regression at every origin.
Within an origin, however, future responses are unknown. Because they supply
lags for later predictors, multistep forecasting proceeds recursively:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Reconstruct the reservoir states from observations available through
\(o\), using the selected feature map and the covariates specified for the
analysis. These give the first-step predictors.
\item Simulate a future response under the fitted forecast law, append it
to the response history, and update the reservoir. Repeat to obtain the
predictors at horizons \(2,\ldots,h\).
\item Repeat over future trajectories and parameter draws, then evaluate
the quantile readout using trajectory-specific predictors or their average,
as specified below.
\end{enumerate}
The response law in step 2 is study-specific. Single-level Q--DESN uses its
AL or exAL forecast law, and the dynamic linear baselines use their
state-space forecast laws. The multiquantile simulation instead constructs
an ordered quantile grid at each step, interpolates its inverse distribution
function, and generates the next response using a uniform probability;
probabilities outside the fitted range use the endpoint quantiles. This
interpolation and endpoint rule are part of that experiment's forecast
procedure, not sampling from a single exAL regression. The streamflow
application infers a latent future reference path from ensemble information
and is described separately.

For the primary multiquantile comparison, let
\(\bar{\vect x}_{o+h}=M^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^M
\tilde{\vect x}_{o+h}^{[j]}\) be the average over model-generated future
predictor trajectories. Readout draw \(b\) is evaluated at this common
average,
\begin{equation}
\text{Forecast location:}\quad
\hat q_{o,h,k}^{(b)}=\beta_{0k}^{(b)}
+\bar{\vect x}_{o+h}^{\top}\vect\beta_k^{(b)},
\qquad k=1,\ldots,K.
\label{eq:forecast-location-main}
\end{equation}
The resulting score intervals describe readout uncertainty conditional on
these averaged future predictors. The supplement compares a second
calculation that retains each draw's own recursively generated predictors.
Neither averaging conditional-quantile locations nor taking their posterior
quantiles generally yields the quantile of a fully marginalized predictive
distribution; the reported summaries are identified by their construction.

\subsection{Forecast scores}

Write \(\vect q^*=(q_1^*,\ldots,q_K^*)^\top\) for the forecasts at
evaluation levels \(p_1,\ldots,p_K\), after any ordering rule specified for
the analysis. The multiquantile simulation applies the projection in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:isotonic-projection-main} to each scored draw; raw mean-grid
and draw-level crossings are assessed before projection.

For a quantile forecast \(q\) at level \(p\), let \(e=y-q\) be the forecast
error and define the check loss function by
\begin{equation}
\text{Check loss:}\quad \rho_p(e)=e\{p-\ind(e<0)\}.
\label{eq:check-loss-main}
\end{equation}
Here \(\ind(e<0)\) is the indicator of a negative error. For a predictive
distribution \(F\) with finite first moment and quantile function \(F^{-1}\),
the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) has the integrated check-loss
representation
\citep{GneitingRaftery2007}
\begin{equation}
\text{CRPS:}\quad \operatorname{CRPS}(F,y)
=2\int_0^1\rho_p\{y-F^{-1}(p)\}\,dp.
\label{eq:crps-integrated-check-main}
\end{equation}
For \(0<p_1<\cdots<p_K<1\) and \(K\geq2\), the finite-grid approximate
CRPS (\(\aCRPS\)) is obtained by applying the trapezoidal rule to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:crps-integrated-check-main} over \([p_1,p_K]\),
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Finite-grid score:}\quad
&\aCRPS_{p_1:p_K}(y;\vect q^*)
 =2\sum_{k=1}^K\omega_k\rho_{p_k}(y-q_k^*),\\
\text{Trapezoidal weights:}\quad
&\omega_1=\frac{p_2-p_1}{2},\\
&\omega_k=\frac{p_{k+1}-p_{k-1}}{2},\quad 2\leq k\leq K-1,\\
&\omega_K=\frac{p_K-p_{K-1}}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:acrps-main}
\end{equation}
The weights satisfy
\(\sum_{k=1}^K\omega_k=p_K-p_1\). Thus \(\aCRPS\) retains the scale of integrated
check loss over the evaluation range rather than representing a full-distribution
CRPS. The evaluation grid is common to the competing methods within each
analysis, not necessarily across analyses.

In the multiquantile simulation, the conditional data-generating process
(DGP) is known. Let
\(\mathcal E=\{(o_\ell,h_\ell):\ell=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{eval}}\}\)
be the \(N_{\mathrm{eval}}\) held-out origin--horizon pairs, and let
\(F_{0,\ell}(y)=\Pr_0(Y_{o_\ell+h_\ell}\le y
\mid\mathcal F_{o_\ell}^{\mathrm{obs}})\)
be the conditional DGP distribution given observed origin history
\(\mathcal F_{o_\ell}^{\mathrm{obs}}\), where \(\Pr_0\) denotes probability
under the DGP. For posterior draw \(b\), let \(q_{\ell,k}^{*,(b)}\) be the
adjusted quantile forecast at level \(p_k\) for pair \(\ell\); the oracle
finite-grid \(\aCRPS\) is
\begin{equation}
\text{Oracle score:}\quad S_{\mathrm{DGP}}^{(b)}
=\frac{2}{N_{\mathrm{eval}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{\mathrm{eval}}}
\sum_{k=1}^K\omega_k
\int\rho_{p_k}\!\left(y-q_{\ell,k}^{*,(b)}\right)\,dF_{0,\ell}(y).
\label{eq:dgp-integrated-score-main}
\end{equation}
Here ``oracle'' refers to evaluating expected check loss under the known DGP.
Common simulated DGP paths approximate the expectation over possible future
responses conditional on each observed origin history. This is separate
from the model-generated trajectories used to average future predictors in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:forecast-location-main}: one calculation constructs a forecast,
and the other evaluates it against the known DGP. The held-out observed
response is not substituted for this DGP expectation. Posterior intervals
summarize draws of the score averaged over all evaluation pairs, rather than
the empirical spread of scores across those pairs. The supplement gives
the draw-specific recursive sensitivity and numerical integration checks.

\subsection{DESN specification and selection}
\label{sec:selection}

Selecting a DESN means choosing the temporal predictors before the reported
regression comparison. Candidates vary the depth \(D\), layer sizes
\(n_d\), lag length \(m\), leak rates \(\alpha_d\), target radii
\(\rho_d\), input-weight scales, reductions \(\mat Q_d\), washout, and
weight realization in Eq.~\eqref{eq:desn-hierarchy}. Each study specifies
which quantities are compared and which are held fixed, together with its
selection observations and criterion.

Gaussian regression on the same DESN predictors supplies an inexpensive
preliminary comparison. A ridge prior first regularizes its mean-regression
coefficients; where specified, the RHS prior from
Section~\ref{subsec:coefficient-regularization}, fitted by VB, refines that
comparison before quantile fitting. This Gaussian RHS--VB fit is a Gaussian
regression, not the median AL regression. Selection of predictors, calibration
of prior hyperparameters, and transfers of numerical starting values have
distinct roles. For a
reference regression \(M\), candidate specifications \(R_1,\ldots,R_J\),
and a selection criterion \(S_{\mathrm{sel}}(R_j;M)\), selection takes
the form
\begin{equation}
\text{Selected reservoir:}\quad \widehat R_M
\in\argmin_{R_j:\,j=1,\ldots,J}
S_{\mathrm{sel}}(R_j;M).
\label{eq:reservoir-selection-main}
\end{equation}
The selected DESN specification is held fixed for the reported posterior
summaries. In the
multiquantile simulation, the Gaussian-DESN comparison and subsequent refinement select
one specification for each mechanism. Its reservoir realization and feature
construction are shared by independent Q--DESN under AL and exAL and
MQ--DESN under AL and exAL. These four regressions
use the same selected temporal representation while differing in likelihood
and sharing across quantile levels. The single-quantile study instead uses
criterion-specific specifications, with historical forecast-based refinement
documented in the supplement; this is not a separate prospective validation
experiment.

Checks of the reservoir states assess finite values and saturation near the
limits of bounded activation functions. Checks of the resulting
predictors assess near-constant or highly dependent columns, effective rank,
conditioning, and variation across reservoir realizations. Together these
assess reservoir behavior under the observed inputs and the regression design, complementing the
study of leaky dynamics and the echo state property: the decay of initial-state
effects under a common input history
\citep{JaegerLukoseviciusPopoviciSiewert2007LeakyESN,
YildizJaegerKiebel2012ESP}.

\section{Simulation Studies}
\label{sec:simulation}

\noindent
Two simulation studies examine quantile forecasting under different temporal
structures. The first follows the error-family comparisons of
\citet{yan2025new}, replacing a static quantile with a linear dynamic state,
and compares Q--DESN with structural dynamic linear models. The second
compares independent Q--DESN and MQ--DESN using a common DESN specification
within each generating mechanism. We use \(p\) for a single probability
level and \(p_k\), \(k=1,\ldots,K\), for several levels fitted together.
The main figures report MCMC forecast results; supplementary analyses cover
quantile-path recovery, a longer evaluation period, and VB.

\subsection{Single-quantile simulation design}
\label{subsec:simulation-single-design}

The first study asks whether Q--DESN can reproduce the forecast performance
of dynamic linear models when the temporal structure is linear. Each
observation-error family \(f\), Gaussian, Laplace, or Gaussian mixture,
has a six-dimensional state containing a local level, slope, and two
sine--cosine harmonic pairs. For each \(p\in\{0.05,0.25,0.50\}\), we
subtract the error distribution's \(p\)-quantile to obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{State evolution:}\quad
&\vect\theta_{t,f}=\mat G_\theta\vect\theta_{t-1,f}+\vect\eta_{t,f},
\quad \vect\eta_{t,f}\sim\Normal(\zeros{6},\mat W_\theta),\\
\text{Observation:}\quad
&y_{t,p,f}=\vect F^\top\vect\theta_{t,f}+e_{t,f}-H_f^{-1}(p),\\
\text{Conditional quantile:}\quad
&Q_p(Y_{t,p,f}\mid\vect\theta_{t,f})=\vect F^\top\vect\theta_{t,f}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:single-quantile-dgp-main}
\end{equation}
Here \(H_f\) is the distribution function of the unshifted error, so
\(\Pr\{e_{t,f}-H_f^{-1}(p)\leq0\}=p\). The fixed matrices
\(\mat G_\theta\) and \(\mat W_\theta\), both \(6\times6\), govern
state evolution and its disturbance covariance; the subscript identifies the
state system, not dependence on a realized state. Following
\citet{WestHarrison1997}, the seasonal components have period 90 and
harmonics 1 and 2, with \(\vect F=(1,0,1,0,1,0)^\top\). Within each
family, one evaluation realization supplies the same latent trajectory and
unshifted errors at all three levels; only the centering constant changes.
The displayed quantile conditions on the current state. After future states
are integrated out, the predictive quantile need not equal this path.

We generate 12,000 observations per family, discard the first 2,000, and
index the retained series from 1 to 10,000. Table~\ref{tab:independent-sample-roles}
distinguishes the fitting and forecast periods. At successive origins,
newly observed responses update the state filter or reservoir history, but
the fitted parameter posterior is retained. Within an origin, Q--DESN
generates unknown future response lags recursively, whereas the dynamic
linear models propagate the updated state distribution
(Section~\ref{sec:forecast}).

\section{Application: Streamflow Quantile Forecast Correction}
\label{sec:data}

\noindent\textit{[The issued-window analysis is being recomputed under the
unit-member likelihood specified below. Its numerical results and displays
in this draft remain from the earlier weighted analysis.]}

Daily river-discharge measurements from the United States Geological Survey
(USGS) provide a local reference for assessing hydrological forecasts.
The Global Flood Awareness System (GloFAS) combines meteorological
information with hydrological modeling to produce historical discharge
estimates and ensemble forecasts for flood early warning
\citep{AlfieriEtAl2013GloFAS,HarriganEtAl2023GloFASForecasts,
CEMS2026GloFASDocumentation}. Their distributions can differ from those
observed at an individual gauge. We study these differences for the San
Lorenzo River at Big Trees, California, using historical USGS and GloFAS
records to learn quantile-specific discrepancies. Combining this regression
with the issued ensemble gives corrected forecasts of reference-streamflow
quantiles.

\subsection{Data and forecasting problem}

Discharge is analyzed on the \(\log(1+\mathrm{flow})\) scale, with flow
measured in \(\mathrm{m}^3/\mathrm{s}\) before transformation. Write
\(y_t\) for transformed USGS discharge and \(g_t^{\mathrm{ret}}\) for the
transformed historical GloFAS estimate. At the forecast origin \(T\),
\GlofasApplicationCurrentOriginDate{}, GloFAS provides 51 ensemble
members for the following \GlofasApplicationCurrentScoredHorizons{} days,
ending on 22~January~2023. Let \(g_{T+h,e}^{\mathrm{ens}}\) denote member
\(e\) at horizon \(h\), on the same transformed scale.

Precipitation predictors come from the Parameter-elevation Regressions on
Independent Slopes Model (PRISM), and soil-moisture predictors from ERA5,
the fifth-generation reanalysis of the European Centre for Medium-Range
Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). Both use realized values during the forecast
window; future USGS measurements are reserved for scoring. The supplement
gives the full historical record and preprocessing.

\subsection{Quantile-based forecast correction}

The historical reference record and the difference
\(g_t^{\mathrm{ret}}-y_t\) provide separate response histories for the
reference and discrepancy regressions. Two fixed DESNs transform these
histories and weather predictors into
\(\vect x_t^{\mathrm{ref}}\in\R^{1501}\) and
\(\vect x_t^{\mathrm{disc}}\in\R^{1001}\), respectively. Each vector
contains an intercept and the states of its selected reservoir; the two
reservoirs can use different lag ranges. The supplement gives their full
specifications and preprocessing.

At probability level \(p\), let
\(q_{p,t}=\vect x_t^{\mathrm{ref}\top}\vect\beta_p\) be the reference
location and
\(d_{p,t}=\vect x_t^{\mathrm{disc}\top}\vect\delta_p\) the
GloFAS-minus-reference location adjustment. The historical and issued
observation models are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Reference history:}\quad
y_t\mid\cdot&\sim\exAL_p(q_{p,t},\sigma_{Y,p},\gamma_{Y,p}),\\
\text{Retrospective GloFAS:}\quad
g_t^{\mathrm{ret}}\mid\cdot&\sim
\exAL_p(q_{p,t}+d_{p,t},\sigma_{G,p},\gamma_{G,p}),\\
\text{Issued member:}\quad
g_{T+h,e}^{\mathrm{ens}}\mid\cdot&\sim
\exAL_p(q_{p,T+h}+d_{p,T+h},\sigma_{G,p},\gamma_{G,p}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:glofas-part4-main}
\end{equation}
AL is obtained by fixing the corresponding shape parameters at zero. The
GloFAS scale and shape are shared by the historical product and its issued
members. Each historical observation and each issued member contributes one
ordinary likelihood factor. The product assumes conditionally independent
residuals across members and horizons, given the regression parameters and
future input path.

Future reference values are unknown but are needed as lagged inputs after
the origin. Write \(\vect y_F=(y_{T+1},\ldots,y_{T+H})^\top\) for this
latent path. Its Gaussian prior is calibrated from recursive forecasts of
the combined historical Gaussian DESN (RHS) fit, without using issued
members or future USGS measurements. This prior supplies the future-path
distribution in the quantile regressions; it is not an additional set of
observations. Given a candidate path, both reservoirs are continued using
its earlier values, and the issued ensemble updates the reference and
discrepancy regressions through Eq.~\eqref{eq:glofas-part4-main}.

The reference forecast is the variational mean of its extrapolated
quantile-location function,
\begin{equation}
\text{Reference forecast:}\quad
\widehat q_{p,h}
=\E_q\!\left[
\vect x_{T+h}^{\mathrm{ref}}(\vect y_F)^\top\vect\beta_p
\right],
\label{eq:glofas-reference-forecast-main}
\end{equation}
evaluated using the latent-path approximation described in the supplement.
This summarizes the level-specific regression location; it is not obtained
by taking a quantile of the latent-path posterior. The Gaussian comparators
instead report quantiles of their simulated future reference paths.

The comparison uses the seven levels \(0.05\), \(0.20\), \(0.35\), \(0.50\),
\(0.65\), \(0.80\), and \(0.95\). Q--DESN fits the levels separately under
AL or exAL; MQ--DESN applies adjacent-quantile RHS shrinkage within the
reference and discrepancy coefficient paths. Gaussian DESN baselines with ridge or RHS regularization and the
raw GloFAS ensemble complete the comparison.

The reservoir and prior specifications were selected using historical
development and confirmation periods, without using the held-out issued
window. Posterior inference then accounts for the regression parameters
and the latent future streamflow path. The long historical record and
high-dimensional regressions make MCMC computationally demanding, so we
use VB to approximate this posterior. The supplement gives the family-specific
updates, latent-path approximation and numerical stopping criteria.

\section{Application: Electricity-Price Quantile Forecasting}
\label{sec:pricefm-application}

Day-ahead electricity prices vary with demand, renewable generation, and
exchange between connected markets. Conditional quantiles describe the
associated price uncertainty. Foundation models use pretraining to learn
representations that can be reused across related series. PriceFM specializes
this approach to probabilistic electricity-price forecasting with regional
covariates and transmission-network information \citep{YuEtAl2026PriceFM}.
Its authors also provide a European benchmark, which we use to compare
independent Q--DESN forecasts with the released PriceFM predictions.
Version~4 contains day-ahead price records and load, solar, and wind forecast
covariates at 15-minute
resolution from 1~January~2022 through 1~January~2026.

\subsection{Comparison design}

The comparison contains \PricefmFullRegionFolds{} region--fold evaluations,
spanning \PricefmFullRegions{} European bidding regions and
\PricefmFullFolds{} rolling folds. For each region, one Q--DESN specification
was selected using temporal validation periods within the first training
interval. The first outer validation period determined the AL or exAL family.
The DESN specification, shrinkage settings, predictors, and AL or exAL
likelihood were then held fixed across that region's three test folds.
Each daily forecast origin has 96 quarter-hourly targets.

Each evaluation is scored on the fold-aligned held-out timestamps and
probability levels \PricefmFullPaperQuantiles. We use average quantile loss
(AQL) to compare forecasts. Throughout this section,
\(\Delta\) AQL denotes Q--DESN AQL minus PriceFM AQL. Negative values indicate
lower Q--DESN AQL; positive values indicate lower PriceFM AQL.
For region \(r\) and fold \(f\), with \(N_{r,f}\) scored timestamps, the score is
\begin{equation}
\text{Average quantile loss:}\quad
\operatorname{AQL}_{r,f}
=\frac{1}{N_{r,f}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{r,f}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\rho_{p_k}\!\left(y_{r,f,i}-\widehat q_{r,f,i,k}\right),
\label{eq:pricefm-aql-main}
\end{equation}
where \(K=7\) and both outcomes and forecasts are in euros per megawatt-hour
(EUR/MWh).
Unlike \(\aCRPS\), AQL equally weights the probability levels and has no
factor of two. Aggregate scores give equal weight to the 114 region--fold
evaluations, rather than pooling all timestamps.

The predictors include the released load, solar, and wind forecast
covariates; spatial specifications also use neighboring-region features or
their summaries. Their issue-time vintages were not independently verified.
The supplement records the fold dates, training-only preprocessing, and
selection history.

\subsection{Results}

The mean Q--DESN AQL is
\PricefmFullMeanQdesnAql{}, compared with \PricefmFullMeanPricefmAql{} for
PriceFM, giving mean \(\Delta\) AQL \PricefmFullMeanDeltaAql{}.
Q--DESN has lower AQL in \PricefmFullQdesnWins{} of
\PricefmFullRegionFolds{} comparisons, but its aggregate loss is slightly
higher. Table~\ref{tab:pricefm-matched-aql-comparison} shows the variation
across folds: Q--DESN is marginally lower in the second fold, whereas
PriceFM is lower in the first and third. The supplement reports regional
comparisons and the historical sensitivity analysis.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Q--DESN retains a regularized linear regression for the quantile location
while using fixed recurrent features to represent nonlinear temporal
dependence. Its posterior concerns the regression, likelihood, and shrinkage
parameters, conditional on the reservoir. This distinction makes the model
accessible through Bayesian regression calculations without treating every
network weight as an unknown parameter. The AL augmentation supports
Gibbs-based computation, while VB makes repeated regression fits practical.

The single-quantile comparison is most favorable to Q--DESN at the median,
with likelihood preferences varying by observation-error family. In the
multiquantile study, MQ--DESN improves the ordering of posterior-mean
forecast grids, especially under exAL, while independent regressions have
lower mean oracle scores in most mechanisms. These results distinguish the
benefit of adjacent-coefficient regularization for quantile ordering from its
less consistent gains in forecast scores.

The applications illustrate different uses of the regression. For streamflow
correction, the reference--discrepancy regression combines gauge measurements
with GloFAS products at one retrospective forecast origin.
\textit{[Streamflow findings will be updated after the unit-member refits.]}
The electricity-price comparison holds each regional
specification fixed across three test folds and has slightly higher aggregate
AQL than PriceFM, with substantial fold and region variation. The
applications retain their stated information sets: realized weather for
streamflow correction and supplied covariates with unverified issue-time
vintages for electricity prices.

Further work should assess variation across repeated simulated series and
uncertainty from reservoir selection and recursively generated predictors.
Multi-origin GloFAS evaluation with covariates available at issuance and
paired PriceFM uncertainty analyses would extend the present comparisons.
